Introduction

Middle East University was founded in 2005 as a university dedicated to postpost-graduate studies, originally named the Jordan University for Graduate Studies. From the outset it attracted an elite group of distinguished academics and professionals — not only from Jordan but from across the Arab world — holding the rank of professor in their fields.

From the beginning the University built a comprehensive strategy around its vision, mission and values, centred on knowledge as power and excellence in specialisation. Its mission emphasises a golden triangle: developing distinguished education within the specialty, community service, and specialised scientific research that serves society and humanity as a whole.

The University has obtained many quality certificates and accreditations from Arab and foreign countries, opening avenues for cooperation with prestigious international universities to develop its educational and research staff.

For these reasons the Deanship of Graduate Studies and Scientific Research prepared this modern guide, which reflects the latest developments in scientific-research methodology at the global level. It is a reference for supervising professors and a guide for students — for preparing the thesis plan and the thesis itself, and for understanding the approval procedures, which begin with the relevant scientific department, pass through the relevant college, and end with the Deanship.

The guide also explains, in clear and simple language, the formal requirements for theses — drafting, categorisation and presentation — alongside the objective conditions: originality of the research topic, definition of its problem and objectives, clarity of methodology, honesty in documentation, and development of the student’s skills in analysis, discussion and interpretation.

From the Dean of Graduate Studies and Scientific Research. The Deanship asks supervising colleagues and their students to adhere to the rules and instructions stipulated in this guide.

3. Contents of the Thesis Plan

This part covers the content of the dissertation plan (Proposal) that the student prepares in consultation with the academic supervisor when registering the dissertation title. The plan contains three chapters:

Chapter One: Background and Problem Statement

  • Introduction: presents the topic, background, variables and justification for conducting the study — preferably no more than two pages.
  • Problem of the Study: clearly presented (theoretically and/or practically), focusing on the deficiencies, limitations, weaknesses or research gap that the study addresses.
  • Study Objective and Questions: objectives stated in a measurable (SMART) manner based on the study problem; questions and/or hypotheses developed per the nature of the topic. If hypotheses are required they must relate to the questions and objectives, formulated using the null hypothesis (Ho) format. Hypotheses are usually limited to experimental, quasi-experimental and correlational studies.
  • Significance of the Study: indicate the parties that can benefit and what the study adds to scientific knowledge (theoretical and practical importance).
  • Study Design (optional): some studies include a study design.
  • Study Terminology (if any): definition of key terms (theoretical and procedural).
  • Study Limitations: defining the time, place and applied area (human, temporal, spatial and thematic boundaries).
  • Limitations of the Study (if any): limitations that constrain generalisation of findings to the study population.

Chapter Two: Theoretical Framework and Previous Studies

  • Theoretical framework: a summary of the topics addressed in the theoretical part, related to the conceptual framework — preferably not exceeding three pages.
  • Previous studies: a number of relevant Arab and foreign studies directly related to the topic, preferably in ascending order of publication year (oldest to most recent).
  • Commenting on previous studies: indicate similarities and/or differences between the current study and prior studies to highlight the gap, what distinguishes the study, and how it benefited from earlier work.

Chapter Three: Study Methodology (Method and Procedures)

This chapter covers the applied aspect of the study and clarifies whether it is quantitative or qualitative. For field studies the student presents the method used, an overview of the study population and sample, the appropriate data-collection tools, and the statistical methods used to analyse data and test hypotheses. For qualitative research the methodology is agreed with the supervisor, within internationally followed methodology.

  • The Research Method: chosen per the research question (quantitative or qualitative; exploratory, descriptive, correlational or causal; content analysis; experimental or quasi-experimental; longitudinal or cross-sectional), with justification.
  • Study Group: size and characteristics of the population; organise a table where possible.
  • Study Sample: the sampling method, size, characteristics and proportion of the population.
  • Study Tools: the data-collection tools, reasons for selecting them, how they were built or developed, and the studies referred to.
  • Validity of the Tool: content validity via previous studies, face validity (arbitrators), construct validity, or other types.
  • Construct Validity (reliability): ensuring comparable results on re-application; tested via Cronbach’s alpha, test–retest, split-half, etc.
  • Study Variables: independent and dependent variables per the method used.
  • Statistical Treatment: the appropriate statistical method for each question.
  • Study Procedures: the sequential steps the researcher follows in implementation.

List of References

The researcher presents the sources relied on in preparing the theoretical literature and previous studies, according to the seventh edition of APA, as described in this guide.

4. Procedures for Approving the Dissertation Plan

1The committee signs the form for approving amendments to the master’s thesis plan (F344).
2The student submits the Master’s Thesis Plan Approval Form (F183) to the department.
3The student submits the Master’s Thesis Plan Project Form (F604) to the department.
4The plan is approved by department and college and submitted to the Graduate Studies Council, including the department/faculty decisions and Forms F344, F183 and F604.
5The Graduate Studies Council approves the title and plan of the thesis.
6The student starts preparing the thesis and may apply for its defence after at least 4 months from the date the plan was approved.

2. Contents of the Thesis

A master’s thesis addresses a research issue related to a specialised field of knowledge. Its objectives and importance should rise to a level that qualifies the researcher for the degree, provided the student adheres to scientific research methodology and scientific and ethical honesty, with accuracy and clarity in formulating the study issue, its elements, hypotheses, procedures, data collection and interpretation.

Although there are common rules for preparing a master’s thesis, each university has its own rules and regulations, and students must comply with their own university’s instructions when preparing their theses.

The general framework of the thesis

The thesis includes four or five chapters, a list of Arabic and foreign references, appendices, and the first pages — separate pages for: title, authorisation, decision of the discussion committee, thanks and appreciation, dedication, index of contents (lists of tables, figures, appendices, and abbreviations if any), and two summaries of the thesis (one in Arabic, one in English).

The First Pages of the Thesis

  1. Title page (I): symbolised by the letter (I) without it appearing on the page. Contains the title as approved by the Graduate Studies Council, the student’s official name, the sole or main supervisor and co-supervisor (if any), and the statement that the thesis is submitted in partial fulfilment of the requirements for the master’s degree in …, followed by the department, faculty, university and date of completion (month and year). The first page is in the language of the thesis; the second page is identical and translated.
  2. Defence Committee Decision page (II): includes the title, date of approval, and the names, scientific ranks, employers and signatures of committee members — with separate layouts for sole supervision and joint supervision.
  3. Authorisation page (III): the form authorising the University to provide paper and electronic copies of the thesis, in whole or in part, to libraries, organisations and bodies concerned with scientific research, upon request — with the student’s name, date and signature.
  4. Acknowledgements page (IV): thanks to the supervising professor, co-supervisor (if any), committee members and all who facilitated the researcher’s task.
  5. Dedication page (V): the student may dedicate the thesis, characterised by simplicity, brevity, objectivity and avoidance of exaggeration.
  6. Index / Table of Contents (VI): prepared to facilitate access to information; may be concise (chapters and titles) or detailed (subheadings of each chapter).
  7. List of Tables (VII): table numbers, content and page numbers; numbered sequentially within each chapter.
  8. List of Figures (VIII): figure numbers, content and page numbers; numbered sequentially by chapter.
  9. List of Appendices (IX): titles of the annexes attached to the thesis.
  10. List of Abbreviations (X, if any): a table of all abbreviations used, arranged alphabetically, appearing after the table of contents and before the abstract. Each abbreviation is shown with its full meaning.
  11. Abstract in the thesis language (XI): includes the title, researcher and supervisor(s), then a summary of the aim(s), method, sample, place, tools, results and most important recommendations — not exceeding 250 words, with no references, figures, tables or examples, and 3–5 keywords.
  12. Translated Abstract (XII): comes immediately after the original abstract, written in sound and clear language conveying the main ideas — not a literal translation. The abstract is single-spaced.
Pages are symbolised by Roman numerals (I–XII) for the preliminary matter. The example title page reads: “This thesis was submitted in fulfilment of the requirements for the Master’s degree in…” followed by department, faculty, Middle East University, and the month and year.

Chapters of the Study

The master’s thesis includes four or five chapters, whose titles vary by topic and methodology. One of two models can be adopted:

The first model

Four or five chapters, where Chapter One is Background and Problem Statement and the final chapter (four or five) is Results and Recommendations. In coordination with the supervisor, the faculty determines the components, titles and division of the remaining chapters per the topic and the availability of sources. Suitable for the arts, Sharia and law.

The second model

Five chapters: (1) Background and Problem Statement; (2) Theoretical Framework and Previous Studies (literature review); (3) Method and Procedures (study methodology); (4) Results of the Study; (5) Discussion of Findings and Recommendations — plus references and appendices. Suitable for science, medical and engineering sciences, administrative, social and computer science, educational sciences and media.

Chapter One: Background and Problem Statement

Introduction (preferably no more than 3 pages); Problem Statement; Study Objectives and Questions (SMART, with Ho hypotheses where applicable); Significance of the Study; Study Design (optional); Study Terminology (if applicable); Study Limitations; and Limitations of the Study (if any).

Chapter Two: Theoretical Framework and Previous Studies

The theoretical framework summarises the conceptual topics. Previous studies are presented (in the thesis model, in descending order — most recent to oldest). After reviewing them, the researcher points out similarities and differences to highlight the gap and how the study benefited from prior work.

Chapter Three: Study Methodology (Methods and Procedures)

Covers the applied aspect: research method, study group, study sample, study tools, validity of the tool, construct validity (reliability), study variables, statistical treatment and study procedures.

Chapter Four: Results of the Study

The researcher presents results per the questions and/or hypotheses; each question is answered independently and each hypothesis tested against the null hypothesis (Ho), using tables, figures or illustrations. In qualitative approaches the researcher uses participants’ words and phrases selectively and objectively to support credibility and lack of bias.

Chapter Five: Discussion of Findings and Recommendations

The researcher discusses and interprets findings scientifically and logically, referring to previous studies, indicating where results agreed or differed, and noting limitations affecting validity or generalisation. The chapter ends with conclusions and a number of recommendations based on the results and related to the objectives and questions.

Tables, Figures & Appendices

Tables

Table titles should be brief and adequate, placed above the table in bold, numbered sequentially, organised in an easy style and including all information needed to understand the table. Notes, sources and references go directly below the table. Tables are placed on or immediately after the page where first mentioned; detailed multi-page tables are preferably fixed in the appendices, and tables are single-spaced.

Figures and Images

Figures (graphs, charts, scatterplots) should be accurate and clear, with x- and y-axis units identified. The figure or image title is brief and comprehensive, placed below the figure. More than one image may appear on a page, arranged logically. Important parts may be indicated with arrows or clear letters. If derived from a reference, the reference is documented in parentheses next to the title. Coloured figures are allowed, but black-and-white images are preferred for printed copies and journal publication.

Appendices

Appendices are placed after the reference list, given sequential numbers in the body, each with its own descriptive title. Examples include: questionnaires (initial and final forms); a list of the entities in the study sample; a list of referees’ names, specialisations and addresses; official letters to related organisations; educational programmes; study plans; tests of all kinds; training programmes; and details of statistical analysis and hypothesis testing.

3. Procedures for Submitting the Thesis Defence

1The student registers six thesis hours in the semester following the semester in which the thesis plan is approved.
2Provide the Deanship of Postgraduate Studies with the student’s monthly follow-up reports according to Form F506.
3After completing the thesis, the student submits it electronically (Word + PDF) with the Plagiarism Checking Form (F609) to the supervisor, who sends it (in Word) to the faculty member in charge of the plagiarism check.
4The plagiarism-check result is sent electronically to the supervisor, with copies to the dean, head of department and Deanship.
5If the result is within the permissible percentage, the student or supervisor submits to the department: No-Objection Form (F226), Acknowledgement & Undertaking (F613), request form (F609) with the result, a soft copy (Word + PDF), and a monthly follow-up report (F506).
6If the result is higher than allowed, the thesis is returned for modification; a second check is free, after which a fee applies for any additional check.
7The department and faculty submit the thesis and forms to the Graduate Studies Council at least three weeks before the proposed defence date.
8The Council approves the defence date and committee and forwards a copy of the decision to the faculty and supervisor.
9Examiners submit the non-supervisor member’s thesis-readiness assessment (F138) to the Deanship at least three days before the defence.

4. Thesis Approval Procedure

1After the defence, the result is acknowledged on the Master’s Thesis Defence Committee Decision Approval Form (F181).
2The Graduate Studies Council approves the outcome of the thesis defence.
3The student submits the amended version within the required deadline, per the Master’s Thesis Amendments Declaration Form (F182).
4The student submits a hard and electronic copy (Word + PDF) to the university library and submits the Master’s Thesis Submission Form (F227) to the Deanship.
5The student submits an authorisation form for extracting research from university theses (F591) to the Deanship.

Citations & Referencing

In-Text Documentation

This part follows the American Psychological Association Publication and Documentation Manual, 7th Edition (APA 7). To document in the text, three pieces of information are needed: the author’s name, the publication date and the page number — e.g. (author, year) or (author, year, page number).

Faculty of Law: students are excluded from in-text documentation; documentation is done in footnotes according to the OSCOLA system.

Three types of in-text citation

  • Quotation: taking information exactly as it appears in the original source, in the same words and phrasing.
  • Paraphrase: restating the information in the researcher’s own language while preserving the meaning.
  • Summarisation: condensing key ideas from several paragraphs or pages; documented like a paraphrase.

Narrative citation

The author’s last name is written outside the brackets, then the year in brackets.

Examples

One author: “Abusaimeh (2022) defined scientific thinking as…”

Two authors: “Saleh & Abu Rumaila’s (2023) study indicated…”

Three or more: “Mousa et al. (2022) point out that…”

Verbatim quotation: Juma (2022) defined scientific thinking as “…” (p. 134).

Parenthetical citation (end of paragraph)

The last name and year are written in parentheses.

Examples

One author: Scientific thinking is characterised by … (Hamza, 2021).

Two authors: … (Nasereddin & Sharabati, 2016).

Three or more: … (Abu Ermila et al., 2021).

Citation Notes & Special Cases

  • Verbatim quotation: from a book, thesis or dissertation, enclose in quotation marks and cite the page number; the page number is not required for a journal or periodical.
  • Multiple references, same paragraph: document in alphabetical order of authors’ names, separated by a semicolon — e.g. (Al-Ahmad, 2021; Al-Mousa, 2023; Al-Hawari, 2020).
  • Same author, same year: add a letter after the year — (2019a), (2019b), and so on; the same applies to foreign references.
  • Newspaper or cultural magazine: e.g. … (Al-Rai newspaper, 2020).
  • Translated work: write the original author’s name (not the translator), followed by the original/translation dates — e.g. (Craig, 2018/2005).
  • Unknown author: e.g. (Unknown, 2019) / (Anonymous, 2019).
  • Unknown date: e.g. (Zeidan, D.T.) / (Chung, n.d.).
  • Qur’anic verses: documented in the body only, not in the reference list.

The Reference List

All sources used by the researcher are documented in the reference list (Arabic and foreign): books, articles, papers published in conferences and seminars, periodicals, scientific journals, databases, manuscripts, master’s and doctoral theses, websites, and so on.

  • Only references used in the body are included; no reference is listed that is not cited in the text.
  • Arabic references begin from the far right; foreign references from the far left.
  • The list is on a separate page titled “References”, centred on the line.
  • References are arranged alphabetically by authors’ last names. For names with an identifier/alias (e.g. Al-Nuaimi, Al-Tawil, Al-Hashimi), order by the third letter after dropping the alias.
  • For foreign references, the letters “Al” in a surname (e.g. Alexander, Alfred, Alshamari) are part of the name and are not dropped. First and middle names are abbreviated to initials (A. M.).
  • Single line spacing between references; no numbering.
  • Titles (book, thesis, paper, journal name) are italicised — not bold or underlined.
  • Multiple works by the same author are arranged by date; same-year works get letters a, b, c…
  • List all authors up to 20, separated by commas, with “and”/“&” before the last author; do not use “et al.” in the list.
  • The first line of each reference is not indented; the second line is indented by 1 cm (hanging indent).
  • It is no longer necessary to write “Retrieved from” before a URL, unless a retrieval date is needed (e.g. updatable web pages).

Reference Examples (APA 7)

1. Book by a single author

Last name, first name. (date). Title of the book (edition if after the first). Publisher.

Gibson, C. H. (2004). Financial reporting and analysis (9th ed.). Tomson.

2. Book with two or more authors

Separate names with commas; place & before the last author’s name.

Kieso, D. E., & Weygandt, J. J. (2007). Intermediate accounting (12th ed.). John Wiley & Sons.

3. Book translated into Arabic

Original author’s last name, first name. (date). Title. (Translator’s name, Trans.). Publisher. (Original work published xxxx).

Poulin, J. (2009). Translation is a love affair (S. Fischman, Trans.). Archipelago Books. (Original work published in 2006).

4. Chapter in an edited book

Last name, first name. (date). Title of the chapter. In editor(s). Publisher.

5. Book or report by a ministry / organisation / body

U.S. Department of Health and Human Services. (1962). Pressure in adults: prediction and prevention. Rockville, MD.

6. Book by an editor

Riesman, S. J. (Ed.). (1962). A style manual for technical writers and editors. Macmillan.

7. Article in a scientific journal

Last name, first name. (year). Title of the article. Journal name, volume(issue), page range. DOI or URL if available.

McCauley, S. M., & Christiansen, M. H. (2019). Learning as language use: A cross-linguistic model of child language development. Psychological Review, 126(1), 1–51. http://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000126

8. Unpublished master’s thesis or doctoral dissertation

Last name, first name. (year). Title [Unpublished master’s thesis or doctoral dissertation]. University. (URL if available).

Misquita, V. C. (1998). Exploration of factors leading to organizational commitment of the subordinate in unionized environments [Unpublished doctoral dissertation]. Illinois Institute of Technology.

9. Thesis / dissertation from a database

Last name, first name. (year). Title [Master’s thesis or PhD thesis, University]. Database or archive name.

Hollander, M. M. (2017). Resistance to authority: Methodological innovations and new lessons from the Milgram experiment [Doctoral dissertation, University of Wisconsin–Madison]. ProQuest Dissertations and Theses Global.

10. Research presented at a conference

Last name, first name. (year, month days). Title of the paper. Conference name, organiser, venue. URL if available.

11. Newspaper / journal article

Last name, first name. (year, month day). Title of the article. Newspaper name. Link.

Brody, J. E. (2007, December 11). Mental reserves keep brain agile. The New York Times. http://www.nytimes.com
